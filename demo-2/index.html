The ray ($R$) can be written in a parametric form, i.e., the position of any point along the ray can be defined as a function of the parameter $t$:

$$R(t) = o + dt$$

In the above equation, $d$ is a vector that has both direction and magnitude. To check if this equation satisfies our assumptions, we can replace $t$ with $0$, and only $o$ will be left on the right-hand side. This means at the beginning when we have not started our countdown, the ray is at the origin. Similarly, after unit time, the ray would reach $o + d$, and so on. Therefore, the parametric form gives us a mathematical way to calculate points on the path of the ray.

If we want to check if a ray hits a sphere, the sphere needs to be written in algebraic form as well. If we consider a sphere with radius $r$ to be centred at $C$, then any point $P$ on the surface of the sphere will satisfy the following equation:

$$||P - C|| = r$$
Fig. 3: Sphere Geometry: A sphere centered at C with radius r. Any point P on its surface satisfies the relation $||P - C|| = r$.

As we have already established using the ray’s parametric form $R(t)$, we can determine at what point the ray will be after time $t$. So, if we assume that after time $t$ the ray hits the sphere at point $P$, then we can substitute $P$ in the above equation with $R(t)$. After this substitution, the equation is:

$$||R(t) - C|| = r$$ $$\iff$$ $$||o + dt - C|| = r$$

For further simplification, we can substitute $o - C$ with a vector $m$:

$$||m + dt|| = r$$

The magnitude of a vector can be obtained by calculating the square root of the dot product of the vector with itself:

$$\sqrt{(m + dt) \cdot (m + dt)} = r$$

To remove the square root, we can square both sides of the equation:

$$(m + dt) \cdot (m + dt) = r^2$$

Next, if we perform the dot product, the equation will look like:

$$(d \cdot d)t^2 + 2(m \cdot d)t + (m \cdot m) = r^2$$ $$\iff$$ $$(d \cdot d)t^2 + 2(m \cdot d)t + (m \cdot m) - r^2 = 0$$

Once we simplify the equation to the final format, we can see it is in the form of a quadratic equation $at^2 + bt + c = 0$, where:

$$a = d \cdot d$$ $$b = 2(m \cdot d)$$ $$c = (m \cdot m) - r^2$$

In algebra, the solution to a quadratic equation is given by the following formula:

$$t = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$

The result of the above mathematical derivation is a standard quadratic equation. This is a moment of practical abstraction. The complex geometric problem of a line intersecting a 3D sphere has been successfully knitted into a simple, well-understood algebraic problem from high school mathematics. The physical world of light and objects has been transformed into an expression of coefficients $a$, $b$, and $c$.

In the above formula, $b^2 - 4ac$ is the discriminant ($\Delta$) part, and based on its value, the quadratic equation can have either no solution, exactly one solution, or two different solutions. See the figures below for these three possibilities in the context of a ray-sphere intersection:
